ABOUT EUXODES

EUXODES is a small browser arcade project: eight games, built with HTML5 Canvas, organized into two modes (CLASSIC and RUSH). There is nothing to download and no account to create - each game opens in your browser and saves your high score locally on your device.

It started as a coding project between two people and grew into the small collection of games you see on the hub. This page explains why the games exist, what they were designed around, and what research actually says about games like these. I've tried to keep it honest, because a lot of what gets written about "brain training" games oversells the evidence. If you spot a mistake or have a suggestion, we'd genuinely like to hear it.

WHY I BUILT IT

I built EUXODES because I wanted something that felt like the arcade games I actually enjoyed playing, but with games I could mess around with and change myself. It started as a coding project, then slowly turned into a small collection of games instead of just one experiment.

I also liked the idea of making games that reward concentration without needing to be complicated. Games like these can help with reaction time, hand-eye coordination, quick decision-making, and staying focused for longer periods. Obviously, playing a game is not suddenly going to turn your brain into a supercomputer, but there is a reason people get better at patterns, timing, and fast responses with practice.

I gave each game a different purpose while building it. Some are mainly about reaction speed, some are about making decisions quickly, and others are more about surviving long enough without making a stupid mistake.

The main reason I kept adding to EUXODES was simple: I wanted to see how far I could take a project that started with one basic game. It became a way for me to practice coding, experiment with game mechanics, and actually build something I would want to play myself.

WHAT THE GAMES ARE DESIGNED AROUND

The games here were designed around a small set of ideas: concentration, reaction speed, quick decisions, timing, pattern recognition, and avoiding mistakes. Each game leans on one or two of these more than the others, which is why they all share a similar look but play very differently.

That is a description of the design intent, not a scientific claim. None of these games has been studied in a lab, and nothing on this page should be read as proof that playing them changes how your brain works.

WHAT RESEARCH SAYS

Most of the research on games and cognition has focused on fast-paced 'action' video games. Studies have found that people who play action games regularly tend to be faster on tasks that measure reaction time and processing speed, without being less accurate (Dye, Green & Bavelier, 2009). Meta-analyses of training studies report moderate average improvements in processing speed, attention, and visuospatial ability after action-game training, though the studies vary a lot in quality and results (Wang et al., 2016).

Other research has looked at puzzle games like Tetris. One study found that higher Tetris scores were associated with better visuospatial working memory, meaning the ability to hold and manipulate spatial information, but not with verbal memory or general IQ (Lau-Zhu et al., 2017). That study was small and correlational, so it cannot show that one causes the other.

At the same time, the evidence is genuinely mixed. Some reviews warn that many training studies have methodological weaknesses, so the size and reach of any benefits remain unclear (Bisoglio et al., 2014). A more recent meta-analysis found a smaller overall effect and signs of publication bias, meaning published studies may overstate the benefits (Smith & Basak, 2023). A large, pre-registered replication also found that playing Tetris did not produce short-term improvement in mental rotation ability (Timm et al., 2024).

One recent analysis suggests that specific features of games, such as time pressure and fast decisions, may matter more than broad genre labels (Smith & Basak, 2023). The games here are built around some of those same demands: fast reactions, quick decisions, tracking several things at once, and staying focused. That does not mean EUXODES has been proven to improve those skills. It hasn't been tested, and this site should not pretend otherwise.

THE EIGHT GAMES AND THEIR SKILLS

Cyber Snake is built around continuous visuospatial tracking and spatial route planning. You steer a moving snake across the grid while tracking several moving objects at once: your own growing trail, drifting firewall mines, and collectible data nodes. Because hitting your own trail, the perimeter, or a mine ends the run, the game demands continuous steering, avoiding hazards, and decision-making under time pressure.

Cyberpunk Tetris involves mental rotation and spatial transformation of falling tetromino shapes, and it relies heavily on visuospatial working memory, reading the board while planning where each piece should go and weighing hold and next-piece decisions. The fall speed keeps increasing, so placement choices have to be made under growing time pressure.

Comet Belt tests speeded two-choice decisions. Your comet flies forward across five lanes, and each wave of asteroids blocks several of them, so you scan ahead and move up or down into one of the lanes that stays open. It requires timing, sustained attention, and deciding when a lane change is actually needed instead of making unnecessary ones.

Void Dash demands timing and rapid visual discrimination. Different obstacle types require different responses, some can be jumped, some only cleared by dashing through them, so you have to identify each obstacle and select the correct response quickly. A limited dash resource and steadily increasing speed add resource management and sustained attention on top.

Reflex focuses on speeded left/right choice reaction. Blocks appear in random lanes and the game speed keeps rising, so it involves visual scanning, sustained attention, and responding accurately under increasing time pressure.

Vector Sentinel is built around continuous 360-degree tracking and precision aiming. You steer a vector craft with full inertia while tracking many moving enemy nodes at once, aiming shots at them, and managing a shield that empties on every hit. Timing an EMP shockwave to clear clusters of enemies adds resource timing on top.

Neon Breakout demands predictive control and anticipation. You move a paddle to return a speeding ball while tracking the position of the remaining blocks, deciding where to hit the ball next. The kinetic charge accelerates every level, so timing and rapid small corrections matter more as you climb.

Neon Tomb demands fast directional decisions under constant time pressure. You slide continuously through an infinite procedural crypt until you hit a wall, so every turn has to be planned several steps ahead. The rising firewall shredder, cycling laser gates, spike pits, and hunting drones force rapid re-planning and quick reactions while you keep moving.

THE HONEST BOTTOM LINE

None of this means EUXODES has been tested in a lab, or that playing these games will measurably improve your reaction time, memory, IQ, or health. The research described above used different games, far more play time, often dozens of hours over weeks, and controlled conditions. A few minutes of arcade play is not a training study, and the findings do not transfer to this site by default.

The research on games and cognition is mixed, and the evidence described here should not be treated as proof that EUXODES provides a medical or cognitive treatment. If you come across claims that games automatically 'train your brain', improve IQ, or treat attention problems, check what the actual study tested before believing the headline.
